Who has the bedrooms facing the street? I will know as soon as you move in. Then I can plan better.
That is a real sentence, from a real letter, sent to a real family. And this one genuinely scared me.
Because everything you are about to hear actually happened. No fiction. No embellishment. Just the facts.
Westfield, New Jersey. June, twenty fourteen. A quiet suburb, forty minutes from New York City.
Derek and Maria Broaddus had three young kids, and they had just bought their dream home.
Six bedrooms. Four bathrooms. A wraparound porch. The kind of house you picture your whole life in.
The price was one point three five million dollars. Nearly every cent they had went into it.
They were still painting the walls when the first letter arrived. A plain white envelope. No return address.
It was addressed, in thick block letters, to two words: The New Owner.
Derek opened it on the front porch. Maria was inside, unpacking boxes. The letter began like this.
Dearest new neighbor at 657 Boulevard, allow me to welcome you to the neighborhood.
How did you end up here? Did 657 Boulevard call to you, with its force within?
My grandfather watched the house in the nineteen twenties. My father watched in the nineteen sixties.
It is now my time. Do you know the history of the house? Do you know what lies within the walls of 657 Boulevard?
Why are you here? I will find out. Welcome, my friends. Welcome.
It was signed with one name. Two words. The Watcher.
They told themselves it was a prank. Some bored neighbor with too much time on their hands.
They even showed it to the police. The police said it was probably harmless. Then the second letter came.
Two weeks later. And this one knew things it could not possibly know.
I see you have flooded 657 Boulevard with contractors, so you can destroy the house as it was meant to be.
Tsk, tsk, tsk. Bad move. You do not want to make 657 Boulevard unhappy.
The Broadduses had hired contractors the week before. Almost nobody outside the family knew that.
Then came the sentence that turned their blood cold. I want you to hear it exactly as it was written.
Who has the bedrooms facing the street? I will know as soon as you move in.
It will help me to know who is in which bedroom. Then I can plan better.
Let that sink in for a second. Someone was mapping out where their children slept.
I have told a lot of scary stories on this channel, and I will be honest with you. This is the one that kept me up.
You can lock your doors at night. You cannot lock out someone who already knows your floor plan.
The letter went on. All the windows and doors in 657 Boulevard allow me to watch you as you move through the house.
If I were upstairs, I would see you. Let me watch. Let me wait.
Do you need to fill the house with the young blood I requested? Better for me.
Was your old house too small for the growing family? Or was it greed, to bring me your children?
Once I know their names, I will call to them. And I will draw them to me.
Derek stopped sleeping. Maria cried every single night. They hired a private investigator.
The investigator dug up something the police had missed. The previous owners had been hiding something too.
The Woods family had lived in that house for twenty three years. Days before the sale closed, they got a letter.
One letter. They never told the Broadduses. They never told a soul. If you love real unsolved stories like this one, hit subscribe, because I have a dozen more coming your way.
The Broadduses sued them for staying silent. The case exploded into national news. And then the third letter arrived.
February, twenty fifteen. Eight months after the first. Experts confirmed the same hand wrote all three.
Where have you gone to? 657 Boulevard is missing you. Perhaps you found something.
Derek and Maria had never spent a single night in the house. A one point three five million dollar home, sitting empty.
The police built a suspect list. And one name kept surfacing, from the house right next door.
The Langfords. An elderly couple. Retired teachers. They had lived at 658 Boulevard since the nineteen sixties.
Their grown son, Michael, still lived with them. Neighbors described him as odd. He mostly kept to himself.
The family mailbox stood directly across the street. The letters carried no postmark. Someone delivered them by hand.
But the police found nothing they could use. No matching fingerprints. Nothing to make an arrest.
Saliva DNA from one envelope matched a woman. The investigator chased that lead for months.
It led nowhere. Every suspect had an alibi, or a lawyer, or both.
Now here is the part that chills me the most. In a way, the house watched back. Let me explain.
In twenty nineteen, drained by legal bills, the Broadduses sold the house for nine hundred fifty nine thousand dollars.
A loss of almost four hundred thousand. The buyers were a family with grown children. No young blood.
And then, reportedly, the new owners received a letter as well. The Watcher was still watching.
The case has never been solved. Not one arrest. Not one confirmed name. Nothing.
Was it a neighbor with a grudge? A prank that spiraled? Or someone who truly believed the house was theirs to guard?
Think about it. Three letters, across eight months, and the writer knew about the contractors, the bedrooms, the kids.
That is not a random prank. That is obsession. That is patience.
I still think about this one every time I pass a for sale sign. And here is the truth nobody likes to say out loud.
Most haunted houses have ghosts. This one had a person. A living, breathing person, studying a family.
And honestly? That is scarier than any ghost story I have ever told.
A streaming series dramatized it in twenty twenty two. But the show invented an ending. The real story has none.
The letters to the Broadduses stopped. Or maybe they just stopped telling anyone about them.
The Watcher never explained. Never asked for money. Never showed a face. Just watched. And waited.
Some say it was about the house itself. Old money in Westfield, furious at newcomers gutting its history.
Others say it was simpler. Jealousy. Someone who could not afford 657 Boulevard, punishing those who could.
I have read every theory out there. None of them fit perfectly. And that is exactly what makes it perfect horror.
The scariest stories are not the ones with monsters. They are the ones with loose ends.
