The payphone at my gas station rang at 3:33 AM. The caller ID showed my mother's number.
My mother had been dead for eleven years. So yes, I answered it.
It was not my mother. It was a man, breathing hard, counting down from ten. At one, he whispered my name.
I am Jack Doyle. Three years I worked nights at Mile 61.
Mile 61 sat off Interstate 40 in the New Mexico desert. Your headlights were the only lights for an hour.
One pump island. One ancient ice machine. And a gray payphone, dead since 1994, bolted to the brick wall.
Everybody knew that phone was dead. The company stopped paying the line years ago.
Then came October the fourteenth, two years ago. Dead quiet.
I was restocking the cooler when I heard it. The payphone, ringing.
I went out with a flashlight. The lot was empty. The phone rang anyway.
The LCD glowed faint green. The number made my stomach drop. I knew it by heart.
My mother's old landline. Disconnected eleven years ago.
I picked it up. Loneliness makes you stupid at 3:33 in the morning.
Five seconds of static. Far away, an engine idling. Then a voice began to count.
Ten. Nine. Eight. He was not talking to me. He was reading instructions to himself.
Seven. Six. Five. He stopped, and the static stretched thin, like the line was holding its breath.
Then, very softly: "Jack Doyle. Lock the door." And the line went dead.
I have told many scary stories here, and honestly, this one genuinely scared me.
I owe you honesty. The Jack in this story is me. I worked that shift. I answered that phone.
I locked the front door and sat behind the counter till sunrise, a bat across my knees.
At 4:47 AM a black pickup rolled in, headlights off, and parked by the payphone. The driver never came in.
He stood in the dark beside the phone, like he was waiting for it to ring. Then he drove off.
I called my boss at dawn. He laughed and said the desert plays tricks.
But at 3:32 the next night I woke on my couch, wide awake, like somebody whispered my name.
At 3:33 my shift started with a ringing phone. The payphone. Again.
This time: NO CALLER ID. I almost did not answer. Then my hand moved on its own.
Static. The engine. But no counting. "Pump four is leaking," he said. "Tell them at the pump, Jack."
"Who is this?" I said. He laughed, a short wet sound, and hung up.
I walked to pump four with the flashlight. The concrete was dark and sticky with gasoline.
I shut the pump down and called the fire department. The manager said I saved the whole building.
A spark near a leaking pump could have turned Mile 61 into a crater. The voice had saved my life.
That is what kept me answering. The voice knew things before they happened.
On the third night: "The blue truck at pump two. Do not let him use the card reader. Take cash."
If stories like this are why you are here, hit subscribe now. I have darker ones.
The blue truck pulled in at 2:10 AM. The driver paid with a card at pump two.
I told him the reader was acting up and took cash. He stared, paid, and drove off cursing.
Two days later the sheriff came about a skimmer on pump two's reader. Planted that very night.
Every call was like that. Warnings. Little rescues. The voice never asked for anything, never explained.
I started keeping a notebook. Every word, every prediction, night after night.
Night eleven: "The woman with the flat tire. Walk her to the payphone. Stand between her and the road."
She was shaking, twenty-two, a car following her for thirty miles. I stood where he said.
Night fourteen: "Do not drink the coffee after two. The creamer has turned." Small things. Human things.
I still think about that detail. The creamer. Why would a ghost care if my coffee was sour?
That night I started talking to him. "Who are you?" I said. For a long time, only the engine.
Then: "I am the man who did not lock his door." The line went quiet like a held breath.
"What does that mean?" I asked. "Thirty years. I have counted down for thirty years. Tonight, I reach one."
My blood went cold. That is what he did the first night. Whisper my name at one.
The register clock said 3:31. Two minutes until the phone rang. My hands shook too badly to write.
I flipped the notebook to the first page. And I saw it. The handwriting on night one was not mine.
Neat looping letters: "Ten. Nine. Eight. He does not know yet. Keep him alive until he does."
I never wrote that. I swear, I never saw those words until that second, by flashlight.
The payphone rang at 3:33, right on time. I ran outside. The lot was empty. NO CALLER ID.
I picked up. "What happens when you reach one?" The engine was closer now, like it was idling in our lot.
"When I reach one," he said, "he walks in. And this time, Jack, you will not be behind the counter."
"What do you mean?" I whispered. "Lock the door. Stand between her and the road. Somebody has to answer."
I turned. Through the glass, the black pickup rolled in, headlights off, parking by the payphone.
And I understood, all at once. The man getting out was me. Older. Thirty years older.
He looked at the payphone, then at me, and mouthed three words: "Do not pick up."
The voice was counting again. Ten. Nine. Eight. The older me shook his head, pointing at the receiver.
I dropped the phone. It swung against the brick. The counting stopped mid-number, like a needle ripped off a record.
The older me climbed into his truck and drove into the desert without a sound. The taillights just faded.
When I picked the receiver up: no engine. No voice. Only the soft hiss of a line dead since 1994.
I quit that morning. Told my boss the desert was getting to me. People quit Mile 61 all the time.
They tore the station down a year later. The payphone went to a landfill.
But here is what still keeps me up at night. Nobody else will say it.
Two weeks ago, at 3:32 AM, I woke in my own bed, wide awake. Like somebody whispered my name.
At 3:33 my cell phone rang. NO CALLER ID. And faintly, in the background, an engine idling.
I did not answer. I sat in the dark and counted along. Ten. Nine. Eight. Down to one.
It stopped. In the silence, a whisper like breath on glass: "Jack Doyle. Lock the door." If that gave you chills, subscribe. The next story is the one I have been afraid to tell.
