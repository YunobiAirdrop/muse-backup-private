My dashcam recorded four hours of empty highway that night, and I have not slept right since I watched it.
In every single frame, I am dead asleep in the sleeper berth behind the cab, mouth open, snoring.
And in the driver's seat — hands on my wheel — there is someone else.
I have watched that footage forty-one times. It has never once looked different.
My name is Dale Mercer. I drive night freight for Halvorsen Logistics.
Three years ago, my rig got broken into at a rest stop outside Camden while I was inside buying coffee.
They took a pallet of electronics and my favorite jacket.
After that, the company made us install dashcams. Front cab, sleeper, and cargo.
Three cameras, recording every single second of every run. Back then, I thought it was a good idea. Protection, you know?
October the ninth. I picked up a load of paper towels in Trenton, bound for Atlantic City.
Dispatch offered me the turnpike, tolls and all. I took the shortcut instead, because I am cheap and I was running late.
County Route 532. Straight through the Pine Barrens. Forty miles of black pines and nothing else.
The Barrens have stories. The Jersey Devil. The ghost lights. Everybody in Jersey knows them.
I did not believe any of it. I was wrong about that.
Mile marker 41. Just after midnight. My headlights caught him on the shoulder.
A man in a gray hoodie, soaked through. Thumb out. Standing perfectly still in the rain, like a statue somebody left there.
Company policy is clear. We do not stop for hitchhikers. Ever.
I kept driving. In the mirror, he did not move a muscle. He just stood there and watched me pass, head turned, tracking me.
Seven miles later, at mile marker 48, my headlights caught him again.
Same gray hoodie. Same thumb out. Same stillness.
That is impossible. Nobody walks seven miles in four minutes. I did the math twice.
I told myself it was a different guy. A coincidence. The Barrens do strange things to tired eyes at one in the morning.
At mile marker 55, he was standing in the middle of my lane.
I slammed the brakes and the trailer jackknifed — and when the headlights steadied, the road was empty.
My heart was hammering so hard the dashcam audio picked it up.
I got out with my flashlight, hands shaking so bad the beam was jumping everywhere. Checked the front bumper. Checked the grille.
Nothing. No blood. No dent. Not even a footprint in the mud.
That is when I noticed the sleeper curtain was swaying.
I never sleep with that curtain open. I always close it before I roll.
I told myself the hard braking threw it loose. I told myself a lot of things that night, and I believed almost none of them.
I climbed back in, locked the doors, and drove.
The CB had been quiet all night. Then it crackled.
A voice said, very clearly, in my own voice: Dale. Do not look back.
I nearly put the rig in the ditch.
Here is the thing about fear. It makes you run a checklist, just to feel sane.
Doors: locked. Windows: up. Sleeper: empty. I checked with the flashlight.
Empty. Of course it was empty.
This one genuinely scared me when I watched it back later, because the dashcam caught what I missed.
On the sleeper camera, at the exact second the CB spoke — the curtain moved. By itself.
I drove the last twenty miles with the cab light burning, hunched over the wheel like a scared kid who thinks the dark is full of teeth.
I made the delivery in Atlantic City at four in the morning.
The dock guy asked why I looked like I had seen a ghost.
I almost told him. Then I saw the trailer seal was broken.
The back doors were standing open. The load was fine. But the doors were open.
I never opened those doors. The seal was cut clean, like with a knife.
Inside the trailer, in the thin dust on the floor, lit by my flashlight, there were footprints. Fresh ones.
Muddy boot prints, walking from the doors to the very back wall — and stopping there. No prints coming back.
The dock guy went pale. He said, man, you picked up a rider.
I did not answer. I was already thinking about the footage.
Back at the motel, hands still shaking, I pulled the memory card, plugged it into my laptop, and watched all three cameras straight through.
Front cab: just me, white-knuckled, talking to nobody.
Sleeper: the curtain moving at the exact second the CB spoke. Nothing else.
Cargo: the doors swinging open by themselves at 1:47 AM. The footprints appearing, one by one.
And then I saw it. In the cargo camera, behind the stacked pallets, for exactly three frames, half a second of video.
The gray hoodie. A face that was just wrong. Too long. Smiling wrong.
I called my dispatcher and told him I was done with night runs.
He laughed. Then he watched the clip I sent him. Then he stopped laughing.
That night, at the motel, I dreamed the CB voice said my name — and I woke up with the TV on, tuned to static.
I still think about mile marker 41 every time I pass a hitchhiker I do not pick up.
Which is every hitchhiker. Now and forever.
Two weeks later, the company sent me the archived footage. Routine backup.
I almost deleted the whole folder without watching a second of it. God, I wish I had.
There was a fourth camera file. One I never installed.
Timestamped 3:33 AM. Labeled: REARVIEW.
My dashcam does not have a rearview camera. My truck does not have one.
The clip shows the sleeper berth. I am asleep. And in the driver's seat — hands on my wheel — the gray hoodie is driving my truck.
At the very end of the clip, the last two seconds, it turns its head toward the camera. Slowly. Like it knew I would be watching.
The face is too long. The smile is wrong. And it says, in my voice: Thanks for the ride, Dale.
I am not saying you should believe me. I barely believe me.
I am just saying: I do not drive nights anymore. And I sleep with every light in the house on.
