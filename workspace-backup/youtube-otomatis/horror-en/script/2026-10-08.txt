Three nights ago, a hiker walked out of the North Carolina woods screaming, because something in there had been calling his name, in his own voice, for six days.
This one genuinely scared me. And I have told a lot of scary stories on this channel.
His name was Elias Ward. Thirty-four years old. An experienced section hiker on the Appalachian Trail.
The Appalachian Trail runs over two thousand two hundred miles, from Georgia to Maine, through fourteen states.
Elias was hiking a seventy-mile stretch through the Great Smoky Mountains. Five days. Alone. Just how he liked it.
Here is how it happened. Day one was perfect. Blue sky. White blazes marking every turn.
Those white blazes, two-by-six-inch paint marks on the trees, they are the trail's lifeline. Follow the white, and you are safe.
At mile nine, Elias reached the first shelter: a three-sided wooden lean-to, with a logbook chained to the wall.
He flipped through the logbook while he ate. Mostly trail names and dates. Then he saw a page that made him stop chewing.
The same name, written over and over, down the whole page. Elias Ward. Elias Ward. Elias Ward.
He laughed it off. Some hiker with his name, messing around. He signed his own entry and went to sleep.
That night, around two A M, he woke to footsteps circling the shelter. Slow. Deliberate. Crunch, crunch, crunch.
He sat up, heart hammering, and called out: Hello? The footsteps stopped. Then a voice answered, his own voice. Hello.
Elias told himself it was a dream. A trick of the tired brain. He pulled his sleeping bag over his head and waited for morning.
I still think about this one sometimes, when the house goes quiet at night. Because here is the thing about the woods. Sound travels strangely out there.
Day two. Fog rolled in thick enough to chew. The kind of fog that swallows the next blaze whole.
At a fork with no sign, Elias found a white blaze painted on a rock, pointing left, off the ridgeline, into a hollow he had never seen on his map.
The map said right. The blaze said left. Blazes do not lie, he told himself. So he went left.
Two hours later, the trail spit him back out at the same fork. The same rock. The same blaze. He had walked a perfect circle.
Only now, there were two blazes on the rock. The second one pointed right, where his map had said to go all along.
His hands were shaking when he took the photo. I will tell you right now, that photo is the reason I believe this story.
Day three brought rain. Cold, sideways rain that turned the trail into a brown river.
Around noon, he reached the second shelter, and someone was already there. A man, sitting perfectly still, facing the back wall.
Hey, Elias said, shaking water off his pack. Rough day, huh? The man did not turn around. He just said: You are early.
The man's clothes were dry. Bone dry. His boots were caked in mud, but the mud was fresh, black, and steaming slightly in the cold air.
Elias asked his trail name. The man said: They call me the Warden. Then he finally turned around. And Elias wished he had not.
Because the man's face was wrong. Not monstrous. Just slightly off. Like a reflection in a spoon. Eyes a fraction too wide.
The Warden smiled and said: The trail likes you, Elias. It does not let go of the ones it likes.
Elias had not told him his name. He grabbed his pack and walked back into the rain. He did not look back. He could feel the smiling, though.
He hiked six more miles in the downpour before his legs gave out. He pitched his tent on a bald, an open grassy mountaintop, and collapsed inside.
At midnight, the rain stopped. And in the silence, Elias heard it. A whisper. Right outside the tent wall.
It was calling his name. Elias. Soft. Patient. In a voice that sounded exactly like his own.
He lay frozen, barely breathing, while the whispering circled the tent. Around and around. Closer. Then further. Then closer again.
And then, silence. A long, heavy silence. The kind that presses on your ears.
Then the zipper of his tent began to move. Slowly. Down. By itself.
Elias screamed and lunged, ripping the zipper the rest of the way, and there was nothing there. Just fog, and wet grass, and the white blaze on a tree, watching him like an eye.
Day four. Elias decided to bail. He would cut across to a road crossing at mile forty-one and hitch back to his car.
But every blaze he passed seemed closer together than they should be. Like the trail was shrinking. Like it was pulling him back in.
He checked his G P S. The little blue dot said he was three miles from the road. He had been walking toward it for four hours.
The blue dot had not moved. He was walking, sweating, burning calories, and the blue dot had not moved an inch.
He sat down on a wet log and did the math out loud, just to hear a human voice. Even if it was only his own.
That is when the whispering started again. Only this time, it was not outside the trees. It was inside his head. Elias. Stay.
He ran. Full pack, blistered feet, he ran down that trail until his lungs were on fire and the world went white at the edges.
He burst out of the treeline onto the road crossing at dusk, and collapsed in the gravel, laughing and crying at the same time.
A pickup truck stopped. An old local man leaned out. You look like you have seen the Warden, he said.
Elias froze. The, what did you call him? The old man just smiled a sad smile. Get in, son. I will tell you on the way down.
Here is what the old man told him. Every few years, a hiker comes out of those woods talking about the Warden. A man who walks the trail but never leaves it.
They say he was a ridge runner, back in the seventies. Died in a storm. The trail kept him. It keeps the ones it likes.
Elias did not sleep that night in the motel. He kept the lights on. He kept the T V loud. He told himself it was over.
Day six. Elias drove home to Asheville. Showered for an hour. Tried to forget. He almost did.
Almost. Because on the seventh morning, he found a white blaze painted on the oak tree in his front yard.
Two inches by six inches. Fresh white paint. Still tacky. In the middle of a suburban neighborhood, miles from any trail.
He called the police. They took a report. They thought it was a prank. Elias knew better.
That night, he heard it again. The whisper. Outside his bedroom window. Elias. Come back. The trail misses you.
He moved apartments twice. Changed his number. The whispering followed. Always at midnight. Always in his own voice.
Three nights ago, his neighbor called the police. Elias was in his backyard at two A M, screaming his own name back at the trees.
When they asked him what he was doing, he said the trees were calling him, and he was trying to drown them out.
The doctors call it a breakdown. Stress. Isolation. They have a word for everything, do they not?
But yesterday, Elias sent me that photo from day two. The rock with the two white blazes.
I zoomed in on it. And my blood went cold. Because behind the rock, half-hidden in the fog, there is a figure.
A man. Facing away. Dry clothes. And on the back of his jacket, I swear this is true, you could just make out a word.
WARDEN.
Elias is in a facility now, getting help. He says the whispering has stopped. He says he feels safe.
But last night, I got a text from an unknown number. No words. Just a photo.
It was my own street. My own house. And on the oak tree across the road, a fresh white blaze.
I have told a lot of scary stories. This one genuinely scared me. Because I do not know what happens next.
If you ever hike the Appalachian Trail, and I hope you do, it is beautiful, remember the rule.
Follow the white blazes. But if a blaze ever points somewhere your map does not, do not follow it.
And if you ever hear your own voice calling your name in the woods, do not answer it.
Do not answer it. Do not follow the second blaze. And whatever you do.
Do not let the trail decide it likes you.
