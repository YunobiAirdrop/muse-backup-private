My name is Marcus Cole, and I drive the last bus out of Harlow Creek every single night.
Route Nine. Departure eleven forty P M. Arrival... well, arrival was never quite guaranteed.
The company calls it the Black Hollow run, named for the mining town at the end of the line.
Black Hollow died in nineteen seventy-four, when the mine caved in and took half the town with it.
Nobody lives there now. Nobody rides there either. Except, some nights... somebody does.
It started on a Tuesday, with fog so thick the headlights looked like they were drowning.
Stop one was empty, like always. Stop two was empty. Stop three was where she got on.
An old woman in a grey coat, clutching a paper ticket yellowed with age.
She paid in coins I had never seen before, heavy and dark, stamped nineteen seventy-four.
She sat in the very back, and she did not make a sound for eleven miles.
At mile twelve, I glanced in the mirror, and my blood turned to ice water.
The seat where she sat was empty in the mirror. But when I turned around, she was still there.
She had no reflection. I told myself it was the fog. I told myself a lot of things that night.
Stop four: a man in a soaked raincoat boarded, though it had not rained in weeks.
Water pooled beneath his seat, black as oil, and it smelled of river mud and rot.
He hummed a song I almost recognized, something my grandmother used to sing at funerals.
Stop five: a little girl, no older than eight, carrying a doll with button eyes.
She whispered to the doll the whole ride, and the doll... the doll whispered back.
By stop six, my hands were shaking so hard I could barely grip the wheel.
A young couple boarded, holding hands, their clothes dusted grey like ash after a collapse.
They stared straight ahead, never blinking, never breathing that I could see.
I counted eleven passengers by stop seven. Eleven passengers on a route that served zero.
That was when I noticed the fare box. It was overflowing with those dark, heavy coins.
Every single one stamped nineteen seventy-four. The year Black Hollow died.
I switched on the radio to steady my nerves, and the announcer's voice froze my heart.
"The search continues for the missing Route Nine bus, lost with twenty-three souls aboard..."
The date on the radio display glowed: October fourteenth, nineteen seventy-four.
Fifty-two years ago. Tonight. This exact night, fifty-two years ago.
I remembered the story then, the one the old drivers told new hires as a joke.
In nineteen seventy-four, a bus just like mine drove into the fog and never came out.
They found the wreck at the bottom of Miller's Ravine a week later. No survivors.
Twenty-three souls. I counted my passengers again. Eleven so far. Twelve to go.
Stop eight: an old miner with a crushed helmet, his lamp still faintly burning blue.
Stop nine: a nurse in a starched uniform, her shoes leaving bloody footprints down the aisle.
Stop ten: three miners, arms linked, singing a hymn in voices like wind through broken glass.
Stop eleven: a teenage boy with a backpack, crying silently, mouthing the words "I'm sorry."
Stop twelve: the last stop before Black Hollow. The doors sighed open to empty fog.
Nobody boarded. But every head on that bus turned to look at me.
Twenty-three faces. Twenty-three pairs of eyes, dark as the mine shaft that killed them.
And then I understood, with a terrible clarity, why the old drivers laughed when they told the story.
They laughed because the new driver always asks the same question, and I asked it too.
"Who... who is driving this bus?" The old woman in the grey coat finally spoke.
"You are, Marcus," she said. "You have always been the driver. Every year, on this night."
I looked down at my hands on the wheel, and then at the windshield before me.
There was no reflection. No shadow. Nothing. I had been dead for fifty-two years.
The memories came flooding back then: the fog, the ravine, the screaming metal.
I had driven this route into the dark, and the dark had kept me, and kept me driving.
Every year on this night, the bus runs again, collecting those who never reached home.
And the driver... the driver can never leave. Unless he finds someone to take the wheel.
The air grew colder, and the lights dimmed, and HE stepped out of the shadows.
The Conductor. Seven feet tall, in a uniform blacker than the space between stars.
His ticket punch gleamed like a row of teeth. His smile was worse.
The Conductor raised his ticket punch, and the bus lights died one by one.
"You wonder who they were," he said. "Look. LOOK. Every soul pays its fare in memory."
The windows turned the color of deep water, and the past bled through the glass.
First, the old woman: I saw her young, laughing, boarding this same bus in the summer of seventy-four.
She was going to see her granddaughter. She never arrived. The ravine took her instead.
Then the soaked man: a fisherman who tried to cross Miller's Creek the night the bridge washed out.
He walked eleven miles in the dark, following headlights he thought were rescue.
They were mine. They were always mine.
The little girl pressed her doll's face to the window, and I saw her birthday party, frozen mid-song.
Eight candles. She never got to blow them out. The cake is still waiting, somewhere.
The young couple: newlyweds, eloping, their whole lives packed into two suitcases.
They held hands through the whole fall. They are still holding hands.
The miner with the crushed helmet tipped it to me, and I remembered his name: Elias Vane.
He stayed behind to warn the others when the shaft started groaning. Nobody listened.
The nurse in the starched uniform had been on her way home after a double shift.
She spent her last hour comforting the crying boy beside her. She never stopped caring.
The three miners sang louder, and their hymn became the sound the mountain made when it broke.
A deep, endless cracking, like the earth itself clearing its throat to swallow them whole.
The teenage boy finally spoke, his voice thin as paper: "I told my mother I'd be home by midnight."
"Tell her... tell her the bus was late." His eyes begged me for a lie I could not give.
One by one, the visions faded, and the fog pressed its face against the windows again.
"Fifty-two years, Marcus," he said, his voice like brakes on wet rails. "You drive well."
"But the route grows long, and even the dead grow tired. Name your replacement."
"One living soul, willing to board. One, and you may finally rest."
I wanted to scream that it was monstrous. But part of me... part of me wanted rest.
Fifty-two years of fog and silence. Fifty-two years of never arriving anywhere.
The bus rolled on toward Black Hollow, toward the ravine, toward the place where I died.
And then, through the fog, I saw it: stop thirteen. A stop that was never on my route.
A real stop. A living stop. A young nurse stood there, exhausted, checking her phone.
Night shift. Just finished. Waiting for a bus that, in the real world, would never come.
The Conductor leaned close, his breath like grave soil. "She is tired, Marcus. So tired."
"Open the doors. Let her rest her feet. Let her ride. Just... let her ride."
My hand moved toward the door lever on its own. Fifty-two years of loneliness pulled at me.
The nurse looked up. Our eyes met through the fogged glass. She smiled, relieved.
She took a step toward the curb. Toward my doors. Toward forever.
And in that moment, I saw myself: the scared new driver, the crash, the endless night.
I saw what I would be giving her. Not rest. Never rest. Only my prison, with a new name.
My hand stopped, shaking, one inch from the lever. "No," I whispered.
The Conductor's smile vanished. The temperature dropped until my breath turned to knives.
"Then drive," he hissed. "Drive until the stars burn out, Marcus Cole."
The doors stayed shut. The bus rolled past the living stop, into the fog, into the dark.
The nurse stared after us, confused, as our headlights dissolved into nothing.
She will tell her friends about the ghost bus she almost boarded. She will never know.
Some nights, when the fog is thick on Route Nine, the last bus still runs. And I am still driving.
