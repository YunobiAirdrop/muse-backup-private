This is the true story of the day a mountain exploded and froze a living city forever.
Two thousand years ago, Pompeii was one of the richest resort towns in the Roman Empire.
Twenty thousand people lived here: merchants, sailors, gladiators, and senators on holiday.
Frescoed villas lined the streets, their walls painted with gods, gardens, and scenes of ancient myth.
Bread baked in great stone ovens, wine flowed in the taverns, and the amphitheater roared with crowds.
From their windows, the citizens could see it every single day: a green, peaceful mountain called Vesuvius.
They farmed its rich slopes and pressed its grapes into wine, and they never once feared it.
You see, no living Roman had ever watched a volcano erupt. The word volcano did not even exist.
Some were packing for a voyage across the sea. Some were planning weddings. Life went on, beautifully ordinary.
Imagine an ordinary morning: market stalls opening, children laughing, the smell of fresh bread on the warm air.
On that last morning, nothing seemed wrong at all. And that is the most haunting part of all.
But the mountain had been whispering warnings for years, and nobody was listening.
Seventeen years earlier, a mighty earthquake had cracked the city walls and toppled its temples.
The citizens rebuilt, as people always do, and slowly they forgot to be afraid.
In the days before the eruption, the wells ran dry and the ground trembled under their feet.
Animals fled the fields in a panic, but the people stayed behind, celebrating the harvest festival.
Vesuvius was not a threat to them. It was simply home.
Everything we know about that day comes from a single man, a seventeen-year-old boy named Pliny.
Pliny the Younger watched the disaster unfold from across the bay, and he wrote it all down.
His uncle, Pliny the Elder, commanded the Roman fleet anchored nearby at Misenum.
Around midday, his mother pointed at the sky. A strange cloud was rising from the mountain.
Pliny described it perfectly: it rose like a great umbrella pine, a tall trunk spreading into branches.
The Elder Pliny ordered his fastest ship made ready, not to flee, but to sail straight toward the danger.
He meant to rescue his friends trapped along the shore, and to witness the marvel up close.
As they crossed the bay, ash began falling on the deck like a black snow.
Pumice stones rained from the sky, the sea itself seemed to boil, and the shoreline had changed its shape.
They landed at Stabiae, where his friend's villa offered shelter from the falling stones.

He was a scholar and a hero, sixty years old, and the mountain had claimed him first.
Across the bay in Pompeii, the city had perhaps eighteen hours left, and no one knew it.
The eruption column climbed thirty kilometers into the sky, higher than any mountain on earth.
Then the wind shifted, and a rain of white pumice began falling on the rooftops of Pompeii.

By midnight the column collapsed, and the mountain unleashed its deadliest weapon of all.
A pyroclastic surge: a wall of superheated gas and ash, faster than any galloping horse.
It struck Pompeii at dawn, hundreds of degrees hot, and it killed in a single breath.
Those who remained died in an instant, frozen in the exact posture of their final moment.

The flesh had rotted away centuries before, but the ash had set hard around every victim.
Fiorelli poured liquid plaster into the voids, and when it set, the dead returned to us.
