D-DAY 1944: THE LONGEST DAY

[beat-001] Ten thousand white crosses stand in silence above the sea. This is where our story ends.

[beat-002] Each marker is someone's son. A young man who never came home from the longest day.

[beat-003] Many were only nineteen. They crossed an ocean to a beach they had never heard of.

[beat-004] Today the beach is peaceful. But eighty years ago, this sand decided the world's fate.

[beat-005] What strikes me most here is not the sadness. It is the gratitude. France never forgot them.

[beat-006] To understand these crosses, we rewind. To one morning in June, nineteen forty-four. ... D-Day.

[beat-007] Never had so many ships crossed the sea at once. Seven thousand vessels. One impossible mission.

[beat-008] Imagine being there. Nineteen, seasick in the dark. And the beach ahead is waiting.

[beat-009] The plan had a code name: Operation Overlord. How do you break into Hitler's fortress Europe?

[beat-010] The man in charge was Dwight D. Eisenhower. One hundred fifty thousand lives rested on him.

[beat-011] The Germans had built the Atlantic Wall. Bunkers, mines, barbed wire. Fifty miles of fortress coast.

[beat-012] Rommel inspected those defenses himself. He knew the Allies were coming. But not where.

[beat-013] The Allies chose Normandy for a clever reason. Calais was the obvious crossing. So Germany guarded it hardest.

[beat-014] Normandy would be hit across five beaches. Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno, and Sword.

[beat-015] Here is what school never taught you. Eisenhower wrote a resignation letter, in case it failed.

[beat-016] He carried that letter in his pocket on the eve of battle. That is real leadership.

[beat-017] But a plan alone would never work. The Germans had to look elsewhere. So the Allies built a trick.

[beat-018] Its name was Operation Fortitude. A fake army, dummy tanks, real radio traffic. Led by Patton.

[beat-019] Double agents fed Berlin lie after lie. The invasion, they swore, would come at Calais.

[beat-020] Hitler moved his best tanks to Calais. Nineteen divisions waiting for an invasion that never came. ... The trap was set.

[beat-021] Meanwhile, the French Resistance waited for a signal. A poem on the radio. Tomorrow, the Allies come.

[beat-022] That night, bridges fell. Rails twisted. France tore up the roads Germany would need. In the dark.

[beat-023] And in England, thirteen thousand paratroopers blackened their faces. They would jump first. Into the night.

[beat-024] Then came the storm. The worst weather in twenty years. The invasion was postponed. One day.

[beat-025] His meteorologist found a narrow window. Thirty-six hours of calm, starting June sixth. Now, or perhaps never.

[beat-026] At four thirty in the morning, Eisenhower stood up. The room fell silent. His order: ... Go.

[beat-027] The largest fleet in history sailed into the night. The Germans were certain: no one attacks in this weather.

[beat-028] Many German commanders were not at their posts. Rommel was home in Germany. For his wife's birthday.

[beat-029] Above the clouds, the paratroopers waited for the red light to turn green. Below, Normandy slept.

[beat-030] Before they boarded, Eisenhower walked among them. One boy asked if he was scared. We are all scared.

[beat-031] At midnight, thirteen thousand men dropped out of the sky. Scattered and lost. ... But they fought anyway.

[beat-032] At five fifty, the sea erupted. Six battleships opened fire. The longest day was here.

[beat-033] Picture this. You are twenty. The ramp is about to drop. Beyond it, an entire army waits.

[beat-034] At six thirty, the ramps dropped. On five beaches at once, the Allies waded ashore.

[beat-035] Utah fell quickly. The British and Canadians pushed inland. But on one beach, the plan collapsed. ... Omaha.

[beat-036] Omaha was a natural fortress. High bluffs. A narrow strip of sand. And one unexpected German division.

[beat-037] The bombing had missed. The bunkers were intact. For hours, Omaha hung by a thread.

[beat-038] But ordinary men did extraordinary things. Officers stood up under fire and shouted: follow me.

[beat-039] At Pointe du Hoc, Rangers climbed sheer cliffs on ropes, into machine-gun fire. ... They destroyed the guns.

[beat-040] The medics never stopped. Under fire, they dragged the wounded to safety. Heroes without rifles.

[beat-041] By noon, small groups had clawed off the beach. The bluffs were falling. Omaha was holding.

[beat-042] When the first flag went up on those bluffs, men wept. Not from fear this time. From relief.

[beat-043] Behind the fighters came the tide of war. Tanks. Trucks. Bulldozers. Highways of steel into France.

[beat-044] And the Germans? They were arguing. The phone lines were cut. Real invasion, or diversion?

[beat-045] Hitler's best tanks sat waiting near Calais. Only Hitler could release them. No one dared wake him.

[beat-046] When the German generals finally understood, it was too late. The sea was full of ships.

[beat-047] Rommel raced back through the night. His Atlantic Wall was breached in five places. In one day.

[beat-048] Inland, the paratroopers linked up with the beaches. French villages woke to freedom. Children ran beside the tanks.

[beat-049] After four years of occupation, France tasted freedom again. Bread. Wine. Strangers who had crossed an ocean.

[beat-050] Back home, the world held its breath. Radio bulletins interrupted every program. Families prayed.

[beat-051] President Roosevelt led the nation in prayer that night. Millions knelt. America prayed as one.

[beat-052] By midnight, it was over. The beaches were Allied. One hundred fifty thousand men ashore.

[beat-053] But the cost was terrible. More than four thousand Allied soldiers died that day. ... Never forget.

[beat-054] Thousands more were wounded. Behind the beaches, surgeons worked through the night by lantern light.

[beat-055] Eisenhower read the casualty lists in silence. Then he tore up the letter in his pocket.

[beat-056] D-Day was not the end of the war. But it was the beginning of the end. The Allies marched to Berlin.

[beat-057] Eleven months later, Germany surrendered. The thousand-year Reich fell in less than twelve.

[beat-058] Paris was liberated. Then Brussels. The map of Europe changed color until the nightmare was over.

[beat-059] That is why those crosses still matter. They are not just graves. They are why Europe is free.

[beat-061] And French schoolchildren still place flowers here. They learn the names. So no one forgets.
