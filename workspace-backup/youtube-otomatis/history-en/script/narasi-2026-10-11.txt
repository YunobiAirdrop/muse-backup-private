## CHAPTER: The Road of Silk
Narrator: For a hundred years, one road had stitched the world together: the Silk Road.

Narrator: Caravans crossed the deserts of Central Asia, carrying silk, spices, and silver from China to Europe.

Narrator: Travelers like Marco Polo had shown Europe the riches of the East — and merchants rushed to follow.

Narrator: The Mongol Empire kept the road safe. They called it the Pax Mongolica — the Mongol peace.

Narrator: But along the trade routes, something else was traveling. Something no one could see.

Narrator: In the burrows of wild marmots lived a tiny killer: a bacterium called Yersinia pestis.

Narrator: It rode in the fur of rats, and in the bellies of fleas, hopping from caravan to caravan.

Narrator: The merchants never noticed. The rats never paid a toll. And the plague moved west, one bite at a time.

Narrator: By thirteen forty-six, it had reached the shores of the Black Sea — and a city called Caffa.

Narrator: According to one famous account, the dying khan ordered a terrible revenge.

Narrator: His soldiers loaded the bodies of the dead into catapults — and hurled them over the walls of Caffa.

## CHAPTER: The World Rewritten
Narrator: The plague was gone. But nothing would ever be the same.

Narrator: With so many workers dead, the survivors suddenly held power. A laborer who once earned pennies could now demand triple wages.

Narrator: In England, the king tried to freeze wages by law. The workers simply walked away to better offers.

Narrator: For the first time in centuries, a peasant could bargain with a lord — and win.

Narrator: The old feudal order, built on obedience and cheap labor, began to crack.

Narrator: The plague also changed medicine forever. Cities learned to separate the sick — and to watch the ships.

Narrator: Doctors began to study the body itself, cutting through centuries of superstition. Modern public health was born in the plague's shadow.

Narrator: With so few hands left, women stepped into workshops, markets, and trades once closed to them.

Narrator: Artists painted the Dance of Death — skeletons leading kings and beggars alike, a reminder that death takes everyone.

Narrator: The Church, which had failed to stop the dying, lost much of its unquestioned authority.

Narrator: What strikes me most about this story is not the death. It is how quickly the world rebuilt itself — and how much fairer it became.
