=== BEAT 2 ===
NARRATION:
To understand what happened, picture the island of Sumbawa — in what is now Indonesia. In eighteen fifteen, it was dotted with small kingdoms, including one called Tambora — with its own king, and its own language, found nowhere else on Earth. The colonial governor at the time was a Briton named Stamford Raffles — yes, the man Singapore is named after — and he later collected eyewitness accounts in his History of Java. The people of Sumbawa had lived beside a quiet mountain for generations. They farmed rice, they traded, they prayed. They had no idea the mountain under their feet was a loaded gun. And on the fifth of April, eighteen fifteen, the gun went off — with a warning shot.

NARRATION:
The first explosion, on April fifth, shook houses hundreds of kilometers away. Ash darkened the sky. But people thought the worst was over. They were wrong. Five days later — on the evening of April tenth — Tambora erupted in full. The blast was heard up to twenty-six hundred kilometers away — people in Sumatra thought they heard cannon fire from some distant battle. A column of ash shot forty-three kilometers into the sky — past the clouds, into the stratosphere. ~ Rafts of floating pumice drifted across the ocean. Ash fell like gray snow as far away as Borneo and Java — more than a thousand kilometers from the mountain. For three days, parts of Sumbawa lived in total darkness. Day became night. And the mountain itself? It collapsed. Where a peak had stood, there was now a crater six kilometers wide.

Tens of thousands more died. Historians now put the total death toll near ninety thousand. ~ And here is the part that haunts me: the kingdom of Tambora was wiped off the map. NARRATION:
Eighteen sixteen. A year after the eruption, the volcanic veil was at its thickest — and the Northern Hemisphere paid the price. In New England, it snowed in June. Frosts killed crops in July and August. People called it "eighteen-hundred-and-froze-to-death." Across the Atlantic, it was worse. Endless rain ruined harvests in Britain, Ireland, France, and Germany. Grain prices tripled. Bread riots broke out. In Switzerland, people ate roots and cats to survive. Tens of thousands starved across Europe. ~ An invisible veil of gas from a mountain most Europeans had never heard of — had stolen their summer. No army did this. No king ordered it. The sky itself had turned against the harvest.

In June of eighteen sixteen, an eighteen-year-old named Mary Shelley was stuck indoors at Lake Geneva — too cold and rainy to go outside. Yes — the most famous horror story ever written was born because a volcano ruined a Swiss summer. Byron himself wrote a poem called Darkness, imagining the sun dying. And the painter Turner? For over a century, nobody connected these dots. Then, in the nineteen eighties, scientists became detectives. NASA researcher Richard Stothers dug through old weather records and ship logs. And deep in the ice of Greenland and Antarctica, researchers found it: a massive spike of sulfur in ice layers from eighteen fifteen and sixteen — a chemical fingerprint of a giant eruption. Tree rings across the Northern Hemisphere showed eighteen sixteen as one of the coldest summers in centuries. In nineteen eighty-four, Stothers published the case in the journal Science: the culprit was Tambora. Later studies — by Clive Oppenheimer, and by Self, Rampino and their team measuring the eruption's gases — confirmed it. ~ A volcano solved from ice, trees, and two-hundred-year-old diaries.
