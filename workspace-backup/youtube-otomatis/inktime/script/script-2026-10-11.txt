=== BEAT 2 ===
NARRATION:
Here are the numbers. In nineteen ninety-one, the economist Juliet Schor published a famous book called The Overworked American. She dug through medieval work records — court rolls, manor accounts, church calendars — and estimated that the typical English worker in the thirteen hundreds worked about one thousand six hundred and twenty hours a year. Sixteen hundred and twenty. Now compare that to today. The OECD — the club of rich countries — reports that the average American worker puts in about one thousand seven hundred and ninety-one hours a year. That is nearly one hundred and seventy extra hours — more than four full working weeks — every single year. So the headline is true. Fewer hours, six hundred years ago. But before you start dreaming about trading your laptop for a pitchfork... think about that for a second.

=== BEAT 3 ===
NARRATION:
So why did they work so little? One word: holidays. And I do not mean beach vacations — I mean HOLY days. The medieval church calendar was absolutely packed. Besides the fifty-two Sundays, there were roughly eighty feast days a year when work was forbidden — saints' days, Easter week, the Christmas season, harvest festivals. Skip church or work on a feast day, and you could face punishment or a fine. So add it up: fifty-two Sundays plus eighty holy days — that is around one hundred and thirty days off a year. One hundred and thirty. More than a third of the year... without working. Think about that the next time you have to beg for two weeks of vacation. But here is the question nobody asks: if they worked so little... why were they so poor?

=== BEAT 4 ===
NARRATION:
And then everything flipped. By the sixteen hundreds, people started working MORE — a lot more. The historian Jan de Vries, in a famous nineteen ninety-four study, called it the Industrious Revolution. And here is the crazy part: nobody forced them. People CHOSE to work longer hours. Why? Sugar. Tea. Coffee. Tobacco. Suddenly the shops were full of new, tempting little luxuries — and families wanted them. So husbands, wives, even children worked extra days to afford the good stuff. Sound familiar? It is the exact same engine running our modern lives: the new phone, the bigger car, the nicer kitchen. Work more, buy more. That deal was signed four hundred years ago — and we are still paying for it.

=== BEAT 5 ===
NARRATION:
But here is the part of this story nobody romanticizes: peasant life was BRUTAL. Yes — fewer total hours. But those hours were spent breaking their bodies. No sick days. No weekends like ours. And harvest season meant dawn-to-dusk labor, sometimes sixteen hours a day under a burning sun — because if the grain rotted in the field, people starved. The historian John Hatcher wrote about harvest workers laboring with terrifying intensity, knowing winter was watching. And the payoff for all those days off? Most peasants were dead by their mid-forties. Childbirth, infections, a bad tooth — things we fix with a single pill — killed them. What fascinates me about this is the trade-off: they had TIME, but they had no safety. We have safety... but we have no time. Neither deal is great.

And here is the modern trap. Those sixteen-hundred-and-twenty-hour medieval workers had no retirement accounts, no health insurance, no weekends at the mall. But they also had no burnout, no hustle culture, no four a.m. email checks. Modern productivity made us richer — and more exhausted. Study after study finds that past about fifty hours a week, human output collapses — we are just warming chairs and calling it dedication. So maybe the real question is not "how do we work less, like peasants?" It is: why did all our brilliant machines fail to set us free? The answer is simpler than you think — and it has nothing to do with technology.
