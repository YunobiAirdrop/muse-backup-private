=== BEAT 2 ===
NARRATION:
To understand how shocking this was, you have to see what existed BEFORE. Around twelve hundred BC, the ancient world was not primitive at all. It was the closest humanity had ever come to a global civilization. Mycenaean Greece. The Hittite Empire in Anatolia. New Kingdom Egypt — the Egypt of the pharaohs at their peak. And wealthy merchant cities like Ugarit on the Syrian coast. These kingdoms wrote letters to each other. We have them — the Amarna Letters — where great kings address each other as "my brother." They traded copper from Cyprus, tin from as far away as Afghanistan, grain, glass, gold. Tin plus copper makes bronze — and bronze was the steel of its day: tools, weapons, everything. One historian, Eric Cline, calls it the first international world system. And then — within a single lifetime — almost all of it was gone.

=== BEAT 3 ===
NARRATION:
Now — the smoking gun. The city of Ugarit, on the coast of modern Syria, was one of the richest trading ports of the age. Around eleven ninety BC, it was destroyed so completely it was never rebuilt. But the archaeologists found something extraordinary: the city's archives — clay tablets — still sitting in the kilns where scribes had left them. And the last letters are terrifying. The final king, Ammurapi, writes that enemy ships are approaching, and begs a neighboring ruler for help. Another letter says the city's troops and chariots are away in Lukka, and the grain ships never arrived. ~ And here is the detail that gives me chills: some tablets were found UNFIRED — the scribes were still writing when the city started burning. Think about that for a second. The last words of a civilization, baked into clay by the very fire that destroyed it.

=== BEAT 4 ===
NARRATION:
So who attacked? Egypt gives us a name — carved in stone. The pharaoh Ramesses the Third, around eleven seventy-seven BC, left a giant inscription at his temple at Medinet Habu. It describes a terrifying confederation arriving from the sea. His words: "The foreign countries made a conspiracy in their islands... No land could stand before their arms." Egypt called them the Sea Peoples — the Peleset, the Shekelesh, the Sherden, the Denyen. Ramesses claims he stopped them in two epic battles: one on land, one at sea — the first naval battle in recorded history. Egypt survived. But here is the problem: nobody knows where the Sea Peoples came from. Were they invaders? Refugees fleeing some OTHER disaster? Pirates? Historians have argued about this for a hundred years. The Sea Peoples might not be the cause of the collapse at all — they might be its VICTIMS.

=== BEAT 5 ===
NARRATION:
And that is where the mystery gets deeper — because the newest research says there was no single cause. Eric Cline, in his famous book "Eleven Seventy-Seven BC: The Year Civilization Collapsed," argues for what he calls a "perfect storm" — a systems collapse. Listen to how many disasters hit AT ONCE. Climate scientists found evidence of the worst drought in three thousand years across the eastern Mediterranean — crops failed, famine spread. There was a storm of earthquakes — one study counts a whole series of quakes shaking city after city. The military historian Robert Drews argued that warfare itself changed: cheap infantry with javelins could now defeat the elite chariot armies the palaces depended on. And the trade network? Tin came from thousands of miles away. When the routes broke down, there was no tin — and without tin, no bronze. No bronze, no tools. No weapons. The whole economy was built on bronze, and bronze simply... ran out.

=== BEAT 7 ===
NARRATION:
Here is what "collapse" actually looked like, and it is worse than you imagine. This was not a slow decline. Archaeologists find a layer of ash and burned rubble — a destruction layer — at site after site, all from the same generation. The Hittite capital, Hattusa, was burned and abandoned — never rebuilt. The Mycenaean palaces of Greece — Pylos, Mycenae, Tiryns — burned with their storerooms full. And then something chilling: the Greeks forgot how to write. Their script, Linear B, simply vanished. For four hundred years, Greece produced almost no written records at all. ~ Think about that for a second. An entire civilization that FORGOT HOW TO READ AND WRITE. The palaces were gone. The scribes were gone. The knowledge was gone. That is not a recession. That is a civilizational hard reset.

=== BEAT 8 ===
NARRATION:
But here is the twist nobody expects: from the ashes came the world we live in. Greece entered a dark age that lasted centuries — but Egypt survived, wounded, never again the superpower it was. And the real winners? The survivors on the margins. The Phoenicians — master sailors from the Levantine coast — built a new trading world. And something even bigger changed: iron. Bronze needed tin from far away. Iron ore? It is practically everywhere. Iron tools and weapons were cheaper, and anyone could make them. Robert Drews and other historians argue this democratized warfare — and power — forever. The Bronze Age elite needed a global trade network to keep their bronze monopoly. Iron broke the monopoly. The collapse did not just destroy a world — it accidentally invented a new one.

=== BEAT 9 ===
NARRATION:
So why should YOU care about something that happened three thousand years ago? Because our world looks EXACTLY like the Late Bronze Age. Think about it: our entire civilization runs on global supply chains. Computer chips from one island. Rare earth metals from a handful of mines. One broken link — a pandemic, a war, a drought — and whole industries stop. Cline himself has said the parallels are uncomfortable. What fascinates me about this is the real lesson: the Bronze Age did not fall to one great enemy. It fell because it was TOO connected, TOO specialized, TOO dependent on a system nobody could control. Complexity was its superpower — and its fatal flaw. The palaces did not see it coming. The question is: would we?
