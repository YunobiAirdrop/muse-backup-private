One in the morning, Christmas Day, nineteen forty five. A house is burning — and five children are gone.

Jennie Sodder counts her children in the snow. Four made it out. Five did not. No one would ever see them again.

By sunrise, the house was a pile of ash. And the five missing children had left no trace at all.

The F B I logged the case and never solved it. Tonight, we reopen the file on the Sodder children.

George and Jennie Sodder were Italian immigrants with ten children, living in Fayetteville, West Virginia.

Christmas Eve, nineteen forty five. The war had just ended, and the Sodder house was full of music and laughter.

George was a successful businessman — and a loud critic of Italy's dictator, Mussolini. That had made him enemies.

Ten children, but only nine were home that night. The eldest son, Joe, was away serving in the Army.

Around ten o'clock the little ones went to bed. Marion, nineteen, stayed up late by the radio.

Marion dozed off on the couch as the radio murmured. Honestly, when I first read what happened next, I got chills.

At half past midnight the phone rang. A woman asked for a name nobody knew, with strange laughter behind her.

Jennie answered. Behind the woman's voice she heard glasses clinking — like a party going on somewhere.

Marion woke too. Downstairs the lights were still burning and the curtains stood wide open — odd, at one in the morning.

Through the window she saw a man outside — standing by the fuse box, in the dark. She thought nothing of it.

An hour later Jennie woke to a crash on the roof — then the smell of smoke. The stairway was a wall of flame.

George and Jennie escaped with Marion and two-year-old Sylvia. George Junior and John climbed out an upstairs window.

But five children were still asleep upstairs: Maurice, fourteen. Martha, twelve. Louis, nine. Jennie, eight. And Betty, five.

Nobody could reach their room. In minutes the whole house was burning, and the five children were trapped.

George ran for his ladder — always leaning against the house. It was gone. Vanished.

He ran to his trucks to drive for help. Both trucks — running fine that very day — refused to start.

Marion sprinted to a neighbor's house to phone the fire department. Every second mattered now.

The fire chief lived close by and saw the flames himself. Yet the fire crew did not arrive until eight in the morning.

Seven hours. By then there was nothing left to save — only ash, and one standing chimney.

The fire chief blamed faulty wiring and closed the case. But an electrician had checked that wiring weeks before — and found it sound.

And here is the thing experts still say today: even crematoriums leave bone fragments behind. House fires do not erase five bodies.

Then the family started finding things. The phone line was not burned through — it had been cut. Deliberately.

The missing ladder turned up at the bottom of an embankment, seventy five feet away. Someone had carried it there.

Both trucks had run perfectly that day. That night, both were dead. Someone had tampered with them.

The water barrel George needed to fight the flames was frozen solid. Coincidence? The family did not think so.

Then they remembered October. An insurance salesman had visited — and exploded when George criticized Mussolini.

His parting words, witnesses said: your house will go up in smoke — and your children will be destroyed.

A coroner's inquest ruled the fire accidental. But nobody ever investigated arson — not once.

Searchers sifted every inch of ash. They found coins, toys, appliances — but not one bone from any of the five children.

Death certificates were issued for all five children — though no bodies had ever been found.

The parents begged for a deeper investigation. Instead, the fire chief bulldozed the ruins — and buried the evidence.

The site became a memorial garden. The parents called it a cover-up. Whatever the truth, the evidence was gone.

So George and Jennie did what any parents would do. They hired a private investigator — and never stopped.

They raised a billboard along the highway: the children's faces, and a five thousand dollar reward.

Tips poured in from across America. Each one was a thread of hope — and most led nowhere.

A woman at a Charleston hotel watched four children arrive with adults at midnight — the very night of the fire.

They left before dawn. The woman never forgot those small faces — they matched the missing children.

A waitress in another town served breakfast to children who fit the descriptions — guarded by adults who would not let them speak.

Then a photograph arrived from Central City, Kentucky — a boy who looked exactly like Louis, now grown.

George drove to Kentucky himself. The boy was gone. Another lead — another dead end.

In nineteen forty nine, a bone fragment surfaced at the site — human vertebrae. The family dared to hope.

The F B I tested it. The bones belonged to someone sixteen to twenty two — too old to be any of the children.

Decades of hope, and every answer slipped away. The surviving children grew up — and kept searching too.

Then, in nineteen sixty eight — twenty three years later — an envelope arrived with no return address.

Inside: a photograph of a man about Louis's age. On the back, five words: I love brother Frankie.

Frankie was the family's nickname for Louis. George was certain. Jennie was not — and the trail went cold again.

So what really happened? Theory one: revenge. George's war against Mussolini had angered dangerous people.

Some whispered the Mafia wanted George punished — and took his children to make him suffer.

Theory two: the children were taken before the fire was ever lit — and the blaze was only the cover.

The cut phone line. The missing ladder. The dead trucks. Honestly? That is not an accident — that is a strategy.

Theory three is simpler: the children were taken in and raised elsewhere — given new names, new lives.

That would explain the sightings — the hotel, the diner, the photographs. Children alive, but unreachable.

The F B I logged the case as a possible kidnapping. But it was never solved — not in eighty years.

Five chairs stayed empty at every Christmas dinner after that. The parents hung five stockings anyway — every year.

The billboard weathered storms for decades. The reward grew to ten thousand dollars. Five small faces watched the highway.

George died in nineteen sixty eight — still believing his children were alive somewhere.

Jennie lived until nineteen eighty nine. She wore black for the rest of her life — and never stopped believing.

In my opinion, this was never a fire investigation. It was a kidnapping — that nobody wanted to solve.

Today, researchers still dig through the files. New theories surface — but the core questions never change.

Who cut the phone line? Who moved the ladder? Who was the man at the fuse box? Who made that midnight call?

Maurice. Martha. Louis. Jennie. Betty. Five names. Eighty years. Zero answers.

No arrests. No remains. No confession. One of America's most haunting unsolved mysteries.

Every Christmas Eve, strangers still leave flowers at the memorial — people who never met the family.

We cover cases like this because someone should remember. Forgetting is the one thing the guilty can hope for.

Someone, somewhere, knows what happened that night. Maybe they are still alive. Maybe they told someone.

Cases this old have been solved by smaller clues — a story passed down, a name remembered.
