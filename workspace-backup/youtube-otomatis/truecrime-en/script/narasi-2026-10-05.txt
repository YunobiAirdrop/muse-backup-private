On Thanksgiving Eve, nineteen seventy one, one man pulled off the perfect crime — and vanished forever. He hijacked a Boeing seven two seven, took two hundred thousand dollars, and jumped into the night. Nobody ever found him. Nobody learned his name. This is the legend of D B Cooper. Some say he died that night in the storm. Others say he lived quietly for decades afterward. The F B I chased him for forty five years — but never solved aviation's greatest mystery. Tonight we follow the evidence: the note, the money, the parachutes, and the ghost. November twenty fourth, nineteen seventy one. Portland, Oregon. A man buys a one way ticket to Seattle. He is calm, polite, middle aged. He wears a dark suit, a white shirt, and sunglasses. The ticket agent reads the name: Dan Cooper. It will become the most famous name never solved. Flight three oh five is a Boeing seven two seven, a short hop north, barely thirty minutes. Cooper boards with a briefcase and a calm smile, taking seat eighteen E at the very back. Six crew members. Thirty six passengers. Nobody knows the man in the last row has a plan. He orders a bourbon and soda. He lights a cigarette. The flight leaves Portland right on schedule. Soon after takeoff, Cooper hands flight attendant Florence Schaffner a folded envelope. She expects a phone number. Instead she finds a note, in calm block letters: I have a bomb in my briefcase. Thinking it is a prank, she asks for proof. Cooper opens the briefcase just enough. Inside: red sticks, wires, and a battery. It looks like a real bomb. Her blood runs cold. Schaffner carries the note to the cockpit. Captain William A Scott reads it in silence. The captain orders her to stay in the cockpit. Tina Mucklow will sit beside the hijacker instead. Cooper is polite to everyone, pays for his drinks, and keeps his cigarette lit. He is ready. His demands are clear: two hundred thousand dollars in cash, four parachutes, and a refueled airplane. He wants the money in a knapsack, in negotiable American currency, and parachutes ready when they land. Seattle air traffic control hears the news. The F B I mobilizes. Everyone is told: cooperate fully. Airline president Donald Nyrop authorizes the ransom payment. No one is to risk the passengers. For two hours, Flight three oh five circles over Puget Sound, waiting while the money is gathered. A local bank produces ten thousand twenty dollar bills. Agents record every single serial number. A skydiving school donates four parachutes. Two front, two back, just as Cooper ordered. At five forty five in the evening, long after sunset, the plane finally lands in Seattle. An isolated spot on the tarmac. Floodlights. Snipers. The F B I watches the airplane's every movement. Cooper demands the passengers stay seated. Tina Mucklow carries the money and parachutes aboard alone. The exchange begins. Thirty six passengers walk free, filing past the hijacker one by one. Cooper keeps the two pilots, the flight engineer, and Tina Mucklow. Everyone else is gone. He inspects the money, then the parachutes, checking every strap and cord like an expert. He orders a new flight plan: Mexico City, with a fuel stop in Reno. Low and slow. The landing gear stays down. The flaps set at fifteen degrees. Slow speed, low altitude. At seven forty five, Flight three oh five lifts off again into the black rainy night. Behind the plane, two Air Force fighter jets follow unseen, tracking the hijacker's every move. Cooper orders the crew into the cockpit, locks the cabin door, and stays alone with the money. He ties the cash to his body, puts on the parachutes, and faces the brutal storm outside. The rear of the seven two seven hides a stairway that opens mid flight. Cooper knows it. He lowers the stairs into the darkness. The wind roars. Rain and freezing air flood the cabin. Around eight eleven, the crew feels a sudden bump — the stairs jerking upward, then settling. Cooper is gone. Somewhere over the dark forests of southwestern Washington, he has stepped into the void. He jumps from ten thousand feet into rain, wind, and pitch black forest, wearing a business suit. The fighter jets see nothing. In Reno, at ten fifteen, the crew finds an empty silent cabin. Eighteen days of searching follow. Helicopters, soldiers, and agents comb the cold wilderness. Nothing. No parachute. No money. No body. The man who robbed the sky has simply ceased to exist. The F B I launches the largest manhunt of its era, questioning hundreds of suspects across America. A reporter misprints Dan Cooper as D B Cooper — and the legend gets its immortal title. Copycats try the trick again. The F A A orders locks fitted to every seven two seven. They call it the Cooper Vane — a small device locking the stairs shut in mid flight. Years pass. Theories bloom. Was he a paratrooper? A pilot? A desperate man with nothing to lose? In February of nineteen eighty, a family on a picnic finds something that changes everything. On the Columbia River shore, three rotting bundles of twenty dollar bills: five thousand eight hundred dollars. The serial numbers match the ransom money. It is Cooper's cash, lost to the river for years. But the money raises new questions. How did it get there? Did Cooper drown in the Columbia? Search teams return to Tina Bar. They dig, they sift, they scan. No more money is found. The remaining one hundred ninety four thousand dollars is never recovered. Not one bill. He left behind two parachutes and his black tie — clues that never led to a name. Cigarette butts were saved — but in nineteen seventy one, no D N A testing existed. The F B I built a profile: ex military, experienced parachutist, calm under pressure. A ghost. For decades, amateur sleuths studied the case. Every clue ended in the same dark forest. One suspect was Richard McCoy, who hijacked another seven two seven just five months later. McCoy demanded half a million dollars and parachuted out. But he was caught within days. Was McCoy also Cooper? Investigators studied the cases. The timelines never quite fit together. Other names surfaced over the years — paratroopers, pilots, criminals. None could be proven. More than a hundred persons of interest were named. The F B I interviewed them all. In twenty sixteen, after forty five years, the F B I quietly closed the case for good. They said they would reopen it only if the parachutes or the money ever surfaced. But the legend only grew. Books, films, festivals — Cooper became an American folk hero. Some admire the daring. Others remember he threatened innocent lives. The debate never ends. Every Thanksgiving, the mystery returns — the one crime America never stopped talking about. So did D B Cooper survive the jump? The evidence says maybe. The forest says nothing. One man, one briefcase, two hundred thousand dollars — and the greatest vanishing act in history. This was Dark Case Files.
