# Angkor Wat: The Megacity Swallowed by Jungle

## Beat 1 — Hook (cold open, the shock)
In the year 1200, this was the biggest city on Earth. Bigger than London. Bigger than Paris. A million people. Then, within 100 years, almost all of them were gone. This is the story of Angkor — the megacity the jungle swallowed.

## Beat 2 — Present day: ruins in the jungle
[OVERLAY: ANGKOR WAT, CAMBODIA — TODAY]
I am standing in front of five stone towers that look like giant lotus buds, rising out of the Cambodian jungle. A thousand years ago, pilgrims crossed the long stone causeway to reach the gods. Today, tree roots crush the doorways, and monkeys scream where priests once chanted.

## Beat 3 — 802: the god-king on the mountain
[OVERLAY: PHNOM KULEN — AD 802 — THE GOD-KING]
But the story starts 400 years earlier, on a misty mountain. In 802, a Khmer prince named Jayavarman II climbed Phnom Kulen and declared himself a god-king. One ruler, one empire, blessed by heaven itself. That single ceremony lit the fuse of Angkor.

## Beat 4 — Suryavarman II builds the temple
Three centuries later, a warrior king named Suryavarman II decided to build a temple worthy of the gods. He chose Vishnu, the preserver of the universe. For some 30 years, tens of thousands of workers raised Angkor Wat, stone by stone. When it was finished, it was the largest religious monument on the planet — and it still is.

## Beat 5 — The true city: a million people
[OVERLAY: ANGKOR — AD 1200 — A CITY OF ONE MILLION]
Here is what most people get wrong. Angkor Wat was just one temple. The real wonder was the city around it. Canals, reservoirs, and wooden houses spread over a thousand square kilometers. Laser scans now tell us up to a million people lived here. In 1200, no city on Earth was bigger.

## Beat 6 — Water: the genius
And the secret of that city was water. The Khmer built giant reservoirs called barays. The West Baray alone stretches 8 kilometers — a man-made sea in the middle of the jungle. A spiderweb of canals carried water to rice fields that fed a million mouths. This was not a city with plumbing. This was a city built out of plumbing.

## Beat 7 — Craftsmanship (narrator reflection 1)
What amazes me is this: they moved millions of sandstone blocks from a quarry 40 kilometers away, without the wheel, without cranes, without steel. Just water, elephants, and a patience I can barely imagine. The stones floated down canals on bamboo rafts, dragged the last miles by elephants and sheer human muscle.

## Beat 8 — The churning of the ocean of milk
Walk the galleries of Angkor Wat and the walls tell their myths in stone. The greatest carving of all shows the churning of the ocean of milk — gods on one side, demons on the other, pulling a giant serpent back and forth for a thousand years to win the nectar of immortality. It stretches longer than a football field, carved by hands that believed every stroke.

## Beat 9 — Peak life (narrator reflection 2)
Standing here 800 years ago, you'd see a thousand boats on the canals, markets overflowing with rice and silk, and royal elephants carrying kings in golden howdahs. The temples were painted red and gold. Music drifted over the water. Nobody looking at that world could have imagined it had less than two centuries left.

## Beat 10 — The trap
But the genius of Angkor was also its trap. The whole city depended on perfect monsoons arriving on schedule, every year. The canals had to be dredged. The dikes had to hold. The reservoirs had to fill. It was a machine of astonishing complexity — and like any machine, it had exactly one way to break.

## Beat 11 — The turn: the rains stop [pause]
[OVERLAY: ANGKOR — AD 1340s — THE MONSOON FAILS]
[pause]
And then, the monsoon failed. Tree rings from the region tell the story in hard science: in the 1300s, brutal droughts struck, lasting not years but decades. The reservoirs cracked into dry earth. The rice withered in the fields. And the god-kings, who claimed to command the heavens, had no answer.

## Beat 12 — Collapse cascades: hunger and war
Hungry people stop believing in kings. The canals clogged with silt because there were no hands left to dredge them. Rival princes fought over the shrinking water. The great machine seized up, piece by piece. The carved stones of those years sound desperate — and then, like the Maya before them, there is only silence.

## Beat 13 — 1431: the sack [pause]
[pause]
Then came the final blow. In 1431, armies from the Siamese kingdom of Ayutthaya stormed the city. The Khmer court fled south to the rivers near modern Phnom Penh, and never came back. The greatest city on Earth emptied in a generation. The jungle waited — patient, green, and unstoppable.

## Beat 14 — The jungle takes over [pause]
[pause]
And the jungle took everything back. Strangler figs wrapped the towers in slow wooden fists. Roots split the carved causeways like fingers prying open a book. Stones that priests had blessed fell one by one into the green dark. Within a century, the megacity had become a rumor whispered by farmers.

## Beat 15 — Never truly lost: the monks stay
But here is what most people get wrong. Angkor was never truly lost. Buddhist monks stayed. They swept the leaves from the steps. They lit candles in the dark galleries. While Europe forgot this place existed, saffron-robed monks kept Angkor Wat alive as a living temple for 600 years.

## Beat 16 — Mouhot, 1860
[OVERLAY: SIEM REAP — 1860 — TOLD TO THE WORLD]
The West only heard the story in 1860. A French naturalist named Henri Mouhot hacked through the jungle with local guides and stood staring at the five towers. He did not discover Angkor Wat — he wrote it down for the world. His journals, published after his death, made the lost city famous on every continent.

## Beat 17 — A century of rescue
Then came a century of rescue. French archaeologists spent decades clearing the jungle, fitting fallen stones back like a giant puzzle, and holding the towers upright with concrete and steel. Without them, much of what you see today would be green mounds in the forest. Restoration is still going on, stone by stone.

## Beat 18 — The lasers [pause]
[pause]
And then came the lasers. Between 2012 and 2015, archaeologists flew over the jungle firing laser pulses through the canopy. The lidar maps revealed the truth: the city was far bigger than anyone dreamed. A vast grid of streets, canals, and ponds sprawling over a thousand square kilometers. The lost city was hiding in plain sight all along.

## Beat 19 — They saw it coming (narrator reflection 3)
What haunts me is that they saw it coming. The last kings watched the reservoirs crack and the canals fail, and they kept building temples to the gods of water, as if stone could summon rain. A civilization of geniuses — and the one cycle that mattered slipped right past them. It makes you wonder what we are missing right now.

## Beat 20 — The warning
Because here is the warning Angkor carries for us. They bet everything on one perfect system, and the climate changed the rules. Today, our own megacities bet everything on systems just as fragile. Angkor is not just a beautiful ruin. It is a message from a million people who learned, too late, that no city is bigger than the water it drinks.

## Beat 21 — Today: the heart of Cambodia
Today, Angkor Wat is the beating heart of Cambodia. It stands on the national flag. It became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1992. Every dawn, thousands gather at the moat to watch the sun rise behind the five towers — and for a moment, the city feels alive again.

## Beat 22 — The digging never stops
And the digging never stops. Every year, archaeologists find new temples under the forest floor, new carvings in the mud, new pieces of the puzzle. Ground radar sees walls where the eye sees only trees. Eight centuries later, Angkor is still revealing itself, one laser pulse at a time.

## Beat 23 — The people endure
The kings are gone. The empire is gone. But the Khmer people endure. Their descendants farm the same rice fields, dance the same dances, and speak the same language their ancestors spoke when the towers were new. The city fell. The people never did.
